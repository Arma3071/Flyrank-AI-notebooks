Title & abstract

What this paper asks, and what it finds

Which pages are under-capturing clicks relative to what their search ranking position should earn, and can that gap be predicted before someone has to notice it by hand? We build a content-level feature table of 132,128 pages from the FlyRank search-intelligence warehouse (trailing 90 days ending June 30, 2026), define underperformance as CTR falling below a position-bucket benchmark fit on a client-held-out training split, and score a random forest against the team's existing hand-written rule on an identical, leakage-checked test split of 16,402 pages. On Precision@50 the rule reaches a perfect 1.00 and the model lands close behind at 0.98 — but that comparison turns out to be less meaningful than it looks, since 82.9% of test-set pages already carry the "underperforms" label, so almost any reasonable top-50 list clears it; the model's ROC-AUC of 0.81 and F1 of 0.91 are the more honest read of what it's actually separating. Along the way, the position curve itself turns out not to be monotonic: pages ranking 1–3 average a lower CTR (0.0025) than pages ranking 4–10 (0.0030), a pattern consistent with SERP-feature click absorption rather than a data error. We report this as decision-support ranking, not a claim that the model beats the rule outright, and flag the label's imbalance as the next thing to fix before trusting Precision@50 on this task.

01 · Introduction

The decision this supports

A content team can review a handful of pages a week. The warehouse holds thousands. Something has to decide which handful.

Today that decision leans on a hand-written rule: compare a page's click-through rate to the average CTR of other pages at the same ranking position, and flag the gap. It is transparent and cheap to compute, but it treats every signal about a page — how stale it is, how much traffic it sees, what kind of content it is — as invisible except for one number: position.

This study asks whether a model that can combine those signals finds a better review queue than the rule does, evaluated the only way that matters here: on pages neither the rule's benchmark nor the model has seen, ranked the way a content team would actually consume the output — top 50 first.

03 · Methodology

Assumptions, features, and how the split works

Label. underperforms_ctr = 1 if a page's actual CTR sits below the average CTR of its position bucket (1–3, 4–10, 11–20, 21–50, 51+) — where that average is computed only from the training split, so the benchmark never sees a page it will later be used to judge. Baseline. the existing rule: score = max(0, expected CTR − actual CTR) × impressions, ranked and read as Precision@50. Features given to the model: position, impressions, word count, days since last update, content type, and the two anonymization-noise fields the warehouse ships to flag thin-sample rows.

  1. Grouped split by client, not by rowContent is split 75/25 by client_hash_id, so no client appears in both train and test — otherwise a model could learn a client instead of learning the underlying pattern.
  2. Benchmark fit on train onlyThe position-bucket CTR average that defines the label, and that the baseline rule scores against, is computed strictly from training rows, then applied unchanged to test rows.
  3. Same split, same metric, both methodsThe rule and the random forest are scored on the identical held-out test set, with the identical ranking metric (Precision@50), so the comparison isn't an accident of different evaluation setups.
  4. Leakage self-auditBefore trusting the result: assert zero client overlap between splits, assert none of the model's input features are derived from the label or from clicks/CTR directly, and print the sample size behind every bucket average used.

What the model actually weighs

Feature importances, random forest (held-out test split, window ending 2026-06-30).

Read alongside the label balance, the top-line comparison is less flattering to either method than it first looks. 82.9% of test-set pages are already labeled "underperforming," so a top-50 list drawn almost at random would land close to that base rate before either method does anything clever — the rule's perfect 1.00 and the model's 0.98 are both, in part, a property of the label, not proof of ranking skill. F1 (0.91) and ROC-AUC (0.81) separate the two more usefully: the model is combining impressions, position, and two anonymization-noise fields (below) rather than reading off a single bucket average, which is where it earns whatever edge it has.

The position curve itself is part of the finding. Fit on the training split across the full warehouse, mean CTR by bucket runs 0.0030 (4–10) → 0.0019 (11–20) → 0.0009 (21–50) → 0.0002 (51+) — falling the way intuition expects. But bucket 1–3 breaks that pattern, landing at 0.0025: below 4–10, not above it. Row-level inspection in the Week-4 baseline rules out a join or aggregation artifact — many high-impression rows at good positions show zero clicks, consistent with AI Overviews and other SERP features absorbing the click before a visit happens. That anomaly is why a flat position-based rule struggles at the top of the ranking, and it's the strongest evidence for why the 1–3 bucket needs a different benchmark, not a "just rank higher" recommendation.

05 · Limitations

Honest framing

  • mixed The label is imbalanced — 82.9% of test-set pages are "underperforming" under this definition — which inflates Precision@50 for almost any reasonable ranking. The rule's 1.00 and the model's 0.98 owe more to that base rate than to either method cleanly separating good pages from bad ones; F1 (0.91) and ROC-AUC (0.81) are the more trustworthy comparison until the label is rebalanced or reframed.
  • mixed The CTR-vs-position relationship is confirmed for buckets 4–10 through 51+, but breaks at 1–3. Treat the top-position benchmark as unreliable until it's refit with a method that accounts for SERP-feature absorption.
  • directional underperforms_ctr is a proxy label — a statistical benchmark, not a verified ground truth that a page has a fixable problem. A flagged page is a candidate for review, not a confirmed defect.
  • observed Everything here is one 90-day trailing window on anonymized, hashed data. Nothing in this paper claims to reveal or prove how Google's ranking algorithm works.
  • mixed Precision@50 is compared on a single 75/25 grouped split. That's one draw from the client population — a repeated grouped cross-validation would tighten confidence in the gap between model and baseline before it's used to justify process change.
  • directional High-impression, near-zero-click pages at good positions are flagged the same as any other underperformer, even when the likely cause (SERP-feature cannibalization) isn't fixable by editing the page. Reason codes and the "wrong if" notes below exist specifically to catch this before a human wastes a rewrite on it.
06 · Recommendations

The action playbook

optimize_ctr Pages the model scores highest for underperformance, with a normal update history and moderate impressions. Rewrite title/meta, review snippet-worthiness, re-test. wrong if: updated in the last 7 days — CTR hasn't had time to respond yet
monitor_serp_feature High-impression pages (>300k/90d) with near-zero CTR at good position. Watch for AI Overview / featured-snippet presence before assuming a fixable CTR problem. wrong if: the click was never available to capture in the first place
monitor Pages the model scores as meeting or exceeding their position-bucket expectation. No action — re-score next window. wrong if: recent volatility means one window isn't representative yet
low-confidence bucket Any page whose position bucket has a small training sample behind its benchmark (see methodology, step 4). Treat its score as noisier than the rest of the queue. wrong if: treated with the same confidence as a well-sampled bucket
